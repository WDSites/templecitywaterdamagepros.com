My Wall Is Soaked and the Carpet Still Won't Dry. What Now?

A wall that feels damp days after the leak stopped, or carpet that stays cool and heavy under a box fan, usually means water is trapped in the pad, the wall cavity or the subfloor. A household fan moves air across a surface and does not pull moisture out of a structure. Temple City Water Damage Pros does water mitigation. We measure where the water is, remove it, and dry the building until the readings say it is done.

When Standing Water Is an Emergency

Standing water is an emergency when it keeps spreading, when it has reached drywall or insulation, or when it came from a drain, a toilet or outside. Clean water from a supply line can degrade into a higher contamination category as the hours pass, so waiting overnight is rarely a good trade. Same-day scheduling is common for active water, and after-hours and weekend calls go to dispatch.

Call (626) 879-0276 and dispatch will ask where the water started, whether it is still running, and what it has touched. Please read the safety points below first.

  • If water touches a panel, outlet or switch, stay out of that room.
  • Cut power only if you can reach the panel while standing on dry ground.
  • For any electrical or gas risk, call your utility or 911 before you call us.
  • If sewage is involved, keep everyone out of the water and let the crew handle it.

Never wade through standing water near live wiring to reach a breaker. No wet floor is worth that risk.

The First 24 to 72 Hours of Water Mitigation

Speed matters because the EPA notes that drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours usually prevents mold growth. The first visit follows a set order.

  • Shut-off and source check: the crew confirms the water is stopped, or helps you find the valve.
  • Extraction: truck-mounted units for large volumes, portable extractors for carpet and tight rooms.
  • Moisture mapping: meters and a thermal imaging camera mark every wet wall, floor and ceiling.
  • Removal of what cannot be dried, such as saturated pad, so the rest can dry.
  • Drying setup: air movers and LGR dehumidifiers placed by room volume and wet area.
  • Daily monitoring: readings logged each visit until materials reach their dry goal.

Cleanup of contaminated debris and sanitizing is a separate step, covered under water damage cleanup. If your home is taking on water from outside, see flood damage restoration.

Structural Drying

Structural drying has one goal, which is to bring every wet material back to its normal dry standard, not just to a point where it feels dry. The crew sets a dry goal by measuring unaffected materials of the same type in your home, then dries until the wet ones match.

Air movers push dry air across wet surfaces and lift moisture off them. LGR dehumidifiers, short for low grain refrigerant units, pull that moisture out of the air so it does not settle back into walls. Wall cavities may be vented at the baseboard so air reaches the framing.

Pin and pinless moisture meters give a number for each material. A thermal imaging camera shows cool, wet patches behind paint and tile, so nothing is missed. Readings are logged on day one and again every day, and the equipment is moved when the numbers stall.

Some materials are removed instead of dried. Saturated carpet pad, wet insulation and drywall that soaked in Category 2 or 3 water are the usual ones, because drying them costs more time than replacing them and risks leaving moisture behind. Equipment comes out only when readings reach the dry goal, often after 3 to 5 days, though dense assemblies can take longer.

Water Categories and Drying Classes: How We Decide

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard gives restorers two separate questions to answer. Category asks how clean the water was, and class asks how much of the structure is wet. The table shows how each answer changes the plan.

Category 1Clean source, such as a supply line or tub overflowDry in place if caught early
Category 2Significant contamination, such as washer or dishwasher dischargePorous materials like pad come out, the rest is cleaned and dried
Category 3Grossly contaminated, such as sewage or floodwaterPorous materials removed, surfaces sanitized, containment used

Either answer can change once walls are opened, so the plan is rechecked at each daily reading.

Signs Water Is Still Hiding Behind the Surface

These signs, often noticed a day or two after the leak, point to moisture still trapped in the structure.

  • A musty smell in a room that looks dry.
  • Baseboards swelling or paint bubbling above the floor.
  • Wall surfaces that feel cool to the touch.
  • A ceiling stain that keeps growing after the leak stopped.

Mold needs moisture, so wet materials left past the 24 to 48 hour window are where growth tends to start. If you can already see growth, that is a job for mold remediation under the S520 standard, and health questions belong to your physician.

Where the Water Usually Comes From

The calls we hear most describe the same few sources. A washer supply hose or valve fails, a pipe bursts under a sink, a toilet supply line leaks, an AC condensate line backs up, a roof leaks after rain, or water collects in a crawl space. Slow leaks inside walls are the hardest to catch.

The source decides the category and the plan. A supply-line failure starts clean, a backed-up condensate pan does not, and a roof leak travels along framing before it shows on a ceiling. Mitigation stops the water damage but does not fix the cause. A plumber or roofer fixes the source, and we coordinate drying around that work.

Dry in Place or Remove: Honest Trade-Offs

Most jobs mix both approaches, such as lifting carpet to dry it while discarding the pad.

Dry in place

Best when water was clean and caught early. It keeps your finishes and cuts rebuild work, but it needs steady equipment and daily readings, and it can fail if moisture hides in a cavity.

Remove and replace

Best for saturated pad, wet insulation and contaminated drywall. It leaves less risk behind and speeds drying, but it adds rebuild work and cost later.

What Mitigation Does Not Do

Mitigation is not plumbing repair, roofing repair or basement waterproofing, and it does not rebuild what was removed. Once the structure reads dry, the next job is water damage repair, where drywall, flooring and paint go back in.
