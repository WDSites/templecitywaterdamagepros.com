When Standing Water Is an Emergency

Standing water is an emergency when it is still rising, when it covers carpet or a hardwood floor, when it sits against drywall, or when it is more than a puddle you can mop in minutes. Carpet pad acts like a sponge, and water wicks up walls by several inches in a few hours. After 24 to 48 hours, the risk of mold goes up, according to EPA guidance on drying wet materials.

It is also an emergency if the water touches outlets, a panel or appliances, or if it came from a sewer line. Stay out, call 911 or your utility for any electrical or gas concern first, and then call us. Storm and groundwater events have their own handling on the flood damage restoration page.

Burst Pipe Water Damage

Most burst pipe calls start the same way. Find the main shut-off valve, usually where the water line enters the house, often at an outside wall, in a garage or near the water heater, and turn it fully clockwise. Then open a low faucet to relieve pressure. Individual shut-off valves under sinks and behind toilets can stop a single fixture without cutting the whole house.

Common sources are supply lines under sinks, washer hoses and valves, ice maker lines and water heater failures. Before anything is moved, photograph the source, the water line on walls and each damaged item. Those photos, plus later moisture readings, help any claim.

Extraction goes first. We pull standing water, then use moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera to find water hiding in walls and under cabinets. If readings show water trapped behind drywall or under cabinet toe kicks, small holes or panel removal let air and dehumidification reach it. Repairing the pipe is a plumber's job, and we will tell you if the leak is still active.

Truck-Mounted vs Portable Extraction Units

Both do the same job at different scale. Truck-mounted units stay outside, with long hoses running in, and they have the suction and lift for large volumes. Portable extraction units work inside, move room to room, and fit stairs, closets and tight spaces. On many jobs we use both.

Pumps handle depth. A submersible pump clears water measured in inches or more, then extractors pull what the pump cannot reach. Carpet can often be extracted in place, with the pad lifted if it is soaked or contaminated. Hardwood and laminate are tricky and may need specialized drying, so tell us what flooring you have.

Wet-vacs from a store are fine for a small spill. Beyond a room or two, they leave far more water behind than you would expect.

What to Do While You Wait

These steps are safe for most households and make the job easier.

  • Shut off the main water valve and, if safe, power to the wet area.
  • Take photos and short videos of the source, water line and damage before moving things.
  • Lift valuables, papers and electronics to dry surfaces.
  • Pull up loose rugs and put foil or wood blocks under furniture legs.
  • Open interior doors and cabinet doors to let air in, unless water came from a sewer.
  • Do not use a household vacuum on standing water, and do not walk through water near live electrical items.

Signs Water Is Still in the Structure

Surface dry does not mean dry. Look and feel for these signs after extraction.

  • Carpet that feels cool or damp underfoot long after the surface looks dry.
  • Baseboards that bow or paint that blisters.
  • A musty smell, which can point to materials still holding moisture.
  • Hardwood that starts to cup or crown.
  • A rising utility bill or the sound of water running when nothing is on.

If any of these show up after the first dry-out, moisture readings tell us where the water went. Lingering damp is also why many jobs flow into mold remediation when drying is delayed.

Options and Honest Trade-Offs

You can extract a small spill yourself with a wet-vac and fans, and for a glass of water on tile that is reasonable. For anything that reached carpet pad, drywall or a second room, professional extraction plus drying is more thorough and often saves flooring. Saving carpet over a clean-source leak is possible. Saving carpet after sewage or floodwater usually is not.

Ripping out materials early costs more up front but removes the moisture source for mold. Leaving wet materials in place can look cheaper and often costs more later.
