Flood damage restoration is the removal of storm, river, groundwater or sewage water from a building, followed by measured drying, cleaning and disinfection of everything that stays. It differs from a quick mop-up because floodwater is usually Category 2 or Category 3 under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, which changes what can be saved. Raw Sewage Coming Up a Floor Drain? Stay Out First

If sewage is rising through a floor drain, toilet or basement shower, treat the whole wet area as contaminated. Do not wade in to check it, and do not run washers, sinks or flush toilets, because that sends more water into the same blocked line. If water has reached outlets, appliances or the panel, or you smell gas, leave and call 911 or the utility before you call us. Cut the power only if the panel is reachable dry.

Sewage backup cleanup is Category 3 work. Crews wear gloves, boots, eye protection and respirators, and they set up containment so the mess does not travel into clean rooms. Extraction units pull out the liquid first, then everything the water touched is sorted.

  • Removed and not kept: carpet, pad, wet insulation, and drywall or baseboard that wicked the water up.
  • Cleaned and disinfected: concrete, tile, framing and other hard surfaces, after the gross soil is scraped and flushed away.
  • Moisture checked: meters and thermal imaging cameras confirm what is still wet before anything is closed up.

Porous materials soaked in sewage are discarded rather than cleaned, because they cannot be reliably disinfected. Antimicrobial is applied only to surfaces that are cleaned first. Fixing the blocked main or broken line is a plumber's job, and we can coordinate around that work.

When Flooding Is an Emergency

Treat it as an emergency when water is still entering, when it covers more than one room, when it reaches outlets or a panel, or when it came from a drain, a creek overflow or the street. Delay is what turns a wet-vac job into a tear-out job. Drywall, insulation and subfloors begin to take on water within hours, and the EPA notes that drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours usually prevents mold growth.

Same-day and after-hours calls are what dispatch is built for. We never promise an arrival time over the phone, but we will tell you what to do in the meantime and what to photograph for your records. Our approach to the drying stage is covered on the water mitigation page.

Category 1, 2 and 3 Water: What Each Means

ANSI/IICRC S500 sorts water by contamination, and the category decides how much material can stay. Water also moves up a category the longer it sits, so a clean supply-line leak can become Category 2 after a day or two.

CategoryTypical sourceWhat usually happens
Category 1 (clean source)Broken supply line, tub overflow with clean waterExtraction and drying. Most materials can be dried in place.
Category 2 (significant contamination)Washer or dishwasher discharge, toilet overflow without solids, sump failureExtraction, cleaning and antimicrobial. Carpet pad is removed, hard surfaces are cleaned.
Category 3 (grossly contaminated)Sewage, river or storm floodwater, groundwater rising through a floorContainment, PPE, removal of porous materials, cleaning and disinfection, then drying.

Storm and groundwater flooding is treated as Category 3 from the start, because it carries soil and whatever the water crossed on the way in.

Basement Flooding Cleanup

Basement water is pumped out first with submersible pumps, then finished with portable extraction units. Depth matters. Deep water against a foundation wall can push on that wall, so on a deep flood we typically lower the level in stages rather than all at once. Contents on the floor are moved or removed before the last inches go.

Drying below grade is slower than drying upstairs. Concrete holds moisture, and a basement has little airflow. Air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and moisture readings on walls and slab guide how long equipment stays, and wet drywall, insulation and carpet are cut out where drying in place will not work.

Restoration is not waterproofing. We remove water and dry the structure, but we do not seal foundations, regrade yards, fix gutters or install drains and sump systems. Those are separate trades, usually a waterproofing or drainage contractor, and you should have the cause looked at once the space is dry. For the follow-up rebuild, see water damage repair.

Crawl Space Water and Whole-House Flooding

Crawl space water is easy to miss and hard to reach. Standing water under the floor wets joists, subfloor and insulation, and the damp air can lift flooring above. Crews pump it out, remove wet insulation, and dry the cavity with air movers and dehumidifiers, with moisture readings on the framing as the finish line.

When water has spread through most of a house, the job is organized by room. Extraction runs first on the lowest, wettest floors, then flooring, baseboards and lower drywall are assessed. Large jobs use truck-mounted extraction outdoors with hoses running inside, plus many air movers. The drying plan is mapped on moisture readings, not guesses, and it is adjusted over several days.

Signs the Flood Damage Is Bigger Than It Looks

Some damage shows up after the water is gone. Walk the area once it is safe and watch for these.

  • Baseboards pulling away from the wall, or drywall that feels soft or crumbles near the floor.
  • Cupped, buckled or lifting flooring, and carpet that stays damp after the surface dries.
  • A musty smell, stains creeping up a wall, or paint that bubbles.
  • Outlets and switches near the waterline, which should be checked before they are used.
  • Water marks on the garage or basement walls well above the floor.

How We Assess and Dry a Flooded Home

The first visit maps the damage. We sort the water category, take moisture readings in walls, floors and framing, and use a thermal imaging camera to find wet areas behind surfaces. The scope and price are explained to you before work starts, and the written scope doubles as documentation if you file a claim.

Then the work follows a sequence: extraction, removal of materials that cannot be saved, cleaning and antimicrobial where needed, and structured drying with air movers and LGR dehumidifiers. Daily moisture checks decide when equipment comes off. The standard behind this sequence is ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any company whether its crews follow it. Small spills and clean leaks are handled differently, as described under water damage cleanup.

Insurance in general terms: sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and standard homeowners policies typically exclude flood. Hiring a Flood Contractor in California

Work worth $1,000 or more in labor or materials needs a contractor licensed by the Contractors State License Board, and you can verify any license at cslb.ca.gov. A written contract should state the total price, payment schedule, scope, timeline and license number. Do not pay more than $1,000 or 10% of the contract price as a down payment, whichever is less.
